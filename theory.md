INTRODUCTION:
The humerus is a long bone of the upper limb extending from the shoulder to the elbow and plays a crucial role in locomotion, manipulation, and tool use—activities that are central to human adaptation and evolution. The humerus is not merely an anatomical structure but an important source of information for understanding biological variation, evolutionary history, functional adaptation, and cultural behaviour in human populations. Anthropologically, the humerus is significant because its morphology reflects both genetic inheritance and environmental influences such as nutrition, physical activity, and habitual behaviour. Variations in the length, robustness, curvature, and muscular attachment sites of the humerus provide insights into sexual dimorphism, population differences, and occupational stress. For instance, males generally exhibit longer and more robust humeri with well-developed muscle markings compared to females, reflecting differences in body size, muscle mass, and division of labour in many traditional societies.
In physical and forensic anthropology, the humerus is one of the key long bones used in the estimation of stature, sex determination, and age assessment when complete or partial skeletal remains are recovered. Measurements such as maximum length, epicondylar breadth, and mid-shaft circumference are statistically correlated with overall body height and sex-specific traits. This makes the humerus extremely valuable in reconstructing the biological profile of an individual, especially in medico-legal and archaeological contexts. From an evolutionary standpoint, the humerus provides evidence of changes in upper limb function from arboreal locomotion in early primates to refined manipulative abilities in modern humans. Fossil humeri of hominins show gradual modifications in shaft shape, head orientation, and muscle attachment areas, reflecting the transition from climbing and brachiation to tool-making and complex manual activities. These adaptations underline the importance of the humerus in tracing the evolutionary trajectory of the human upper limb.
Cultural anthropology also finds relevance in the study of the humerus through activity-related skeletal changes. Repetitive actions such as farming, hunting, pottery making, or carrying loads leave biomechanical signatures on the humerus in the form of asymmetry, increased robusticity, or enthesial changes. Such skeletal markers help anthropologists reconstruct past lifestyles, subsistence strategies, and division of labour within ancient populations. Thus, the humerus occupies a central place in research as it integrates biological, evolutionary, and cultural dimensions of human life. Its study contributes to a deeper understanding of human diversity, adaptation, and the complex interaction between biology and culture across time and space.
